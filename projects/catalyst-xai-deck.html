The problem

The paper my lab was reading — Shin et al., “Discovering the Origin of Catalyst Performance and Degradation of Electrochemical CO₂ Reduction through Interpretable Machine Learning” (ACS Catalysis, 2025) — asks a practical question: when a CO₂-reduction catalyst starts failing, is the catalyst itself losing active sites, or is something around it, like blocked pores, starving it of CO₂?

Their answer is a 1-D convolutional network that reads a pair of voltage sweeps (one under CO₂, one under N₂) and predicts current and product efficiency. Grad-CAM then shows which part of the voltage axis the network was looking at, and that location points to the cause. It is a clever argument, but on paper it is spread across four dense figures, and each one reads as its own story. I wanted my lab to follow one catalyst from the moment it is mounted in the cell to the moment someone can say why it died.

What it does

The deck is a navigable 3D walkthrough in five chapters. You fly through the electrolyser cell and the network, scrub a catalyst through thousands of degradation cycles, hover over a UMAP of 5,196 sweeps, and step through the backward pass that produces the attention map. It ends where the paper ends: on the electron microscopy that checks whether the cheap answer was true.

The diagnostic rule the deck builds toward
Attention tracks the boundary
The kinetic/transport boundary is moving: the catalyst is losing active sites
Attention sits under it
The transport ceiling is collapsing: pores or gas access are failing
Then check
Microscopy and spectroscopy confirm or reject the reading

Key decisions

01
No figure from the paper is reproduced

Every curve, attention map and scatter point is generated live from a Butler–Volmer plus limiting-current model, with parameters tuned to the ranges the paper reports. That forced the deck to reproduce the paper’s findings from physics rather than by tracing: silver keeps its diffusion-limited region while its transport ceiling collapses, and Ni–N/C loses its kinetic/diffusion transition entirely as its Tafel slope blows up. The parity plots are sampled so their error matches the reported values (0.0032 A, 0.47 %, 0.25 %).

02
Every claim is labelled: reported, my reading, or not stated

Before building any scene I reconciled my handwritten notes against the paper in a written reasoning record, tagging each statement as reported in the paper, my interpretation, or missing. The deck carries those tags through. When a viewer sees a conclusion, they can tell whether the authors said it or I inferred it — the one line in a paper review that must never blur.

03
One experiment, not four figures

The first version followed the paper figure by figure, and the first chapter ended up summarising the other three before they had earned it. I restructured the flow around a single question and a single catalyst, so each chapter answers the doubt the previous one raised.

04
Questions for the lab, made visible

Two things I flagged in my reading became scenes. First, data leakage: consecutive cycles are twenty minutes apart on the same electrode, so their sweeps are near-duplicates. A random train/test split puts a curve’s near-twin on the other side, which flatters the score; the paper reports split sizes but not how curves were assigned. Second, in the Grad-CAM step the channel weights come from positive gradients and the activations are already non-negative, so the final ReLU has nothing left to clip. Both are my reading, labelled as such, and offered as questions rather than corrections.

How it was built

I built the deck with an AI coding assistant over several sessions, one figure per phase. My part was the reading: working through the paper and its supplement, deciding what each scene had to prove, setting the claim labels, and checking every scene against the paper before it stayed in.
