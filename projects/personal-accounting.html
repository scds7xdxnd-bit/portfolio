The problem

My money moves in three currencies, across bank accounts, scholarships and small loans between friends. Budgeting apps are good at one question — what did I spend on food this month? — and bad at the ones I actually care about: what do I own, what do I owe, who owes me, and will my balance stay above zero until the next stipend lands?

Those are accounting questions, and accounting already has the answer: double-entry bookkeeping, where every movement of money is recorded twice and the books must balance. So instead of tracking spending, I built a ledger.

What it does

Three jobs, one ledger
Record
Journal entries with balanced debit and credit lines, a trial balance, and multi-currency accounts
Import
Bank statements come in as CSV; the app suggests accounts and descriptions and learns from my corrections
Forecast
A money schedule projects cash flow, with a Monte Carlo band and the daily chance of dropping below a floor

It also handles the human side of money: loans between friends get a signed acknowledgement document, a repayment schedule and a running statement, so nobody has to remember who owes what.

Key decisions

01
The journal is the only source of truth

Early versions stored simple transactions. Moving to a proper journal (entry headers plus balanced lines) meant migrating years of history without breaking anything, so the app ran in a dual-write mode that wrote both formats atomically until the journal could be trusted. Reports now read only from the journal; mixing old and new data in one report is forbidden by a test.

02
The app refuses to run on a database it doesn’t trust

Before any page loads, a startup gate checks that the database is at the latest migration and actually has the tables and columns the code needs. If not, it answers with a clear error instead of quietly writing half-correct data. For a system holding real money, failing loudly beats failing silently.

03
Importing the same statement twice is safe

Every import records the file’s fingerprint and a key for each row (date, amount, account, description). Uploading a statement again, or one that overlaps last month’s, skips what is already in the books. Double-counting is the most common way a ledger stops being believable.

04
Suggestions that learn without retraining

A model trained on my own ledger suggests which accounts a new entry should hit; on a held-out slice of my KRW history, the right account is in its top three suggestions about 91% of the time. Only about one entry in six ever came through a bank import, so the description suggester also learns live: every suggestion I accept or edit updates its memory immediately.

05
Monte Carlo only where an approximation was documented

The cash-flow forecast originally used an approximation the code itself labelled as one. I replaced it with a Monte Carlo simulation that draws a fan of likely balances and the chance, per day, of falling below a safety floor — and wrote down where a Bayesian layer earned its place and where it would just be decoration.

How it was built

Much of the recent code was written with AI coding agents working from specifications I wrote. My part is the accounting design, the written contracts every change must satisfy, and a release gate of automated tests that blocks anything which breaks them. That gate is what lets a one-person project change quickly without corrupting five years of records.
