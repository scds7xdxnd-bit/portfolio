Why Gibbs energy, and not enthalpy or entropy alone?

This is the question students rarely ask, and lectures rarely answer. At constant T and P — the conditions of virtually every chemical process and laboratory experiment — the Gibbs energy is minimizedThis follows from the second law. At constant T and P, the criterion for spontaneous change is dG ≤ 0. The system moves toward lower G until it reaches a minimum, which is the equilibrium state. at equilibrium. This is not a definition; it follows from the second law.

Enthalpy alone is insufficient because it ignores entropy — a purely exothermic process can still be non-spontaneous at high temperatures. Entropy alone is insufficient because it ignores energy — a purely disordering process can be non-spontaneous at low temperatures. Gibbs energy combines both through G = H − TS, and at any given T, it correctly identifies which direction change will proceed.

Consider water at 80°C in contact with water vapor at partial pressure P. The chemical potential of liquid water is μliq and that of the vapor is μvap. If μliq > μvap, molecules leave the liquid phase — evaporation occurs spontaneously. If μliq = μvap, the system is at phase equilibrium — the rate of evaporation equals condensation. The equilibrium vapor pressure at any T is precisely the pressure at which these two chemical potentials equalize.

Where the analogy breaks down

The analogy between temperature, pressure, and chemical potential is pedagogically valuable, but it is imperfect in specific ways. Knowing where it breaks down matters more than knowing the analogy itself.

Flaw 1 — Not directly measurable
T and P are directly measurable with instruments. μᵢ is not — it must be inferred from measurable quantities like composition, temperature, and equations of state or activity models.
Flaw 2 — Composition dependence
T and P are scalar fields in space. μᵢ depends on the local composition of the mixture, not just on position. This makes the "field" picture of chemical potential considerably more complex.
Flaw 3 — Coupled, not independent
In a mixture of N species, the N chemical potentials are not independent — the Gibbs-Duhem equation constrains them. You cannot vary μᵢ for one species without affecting others at constant T and P.
Flaw 4 — Sign and scale are non-intuitive
Chemical potentials for real solutions are typically large negative numbers. The absolute value has no direct physical meaning; only differences and equalities matter. This is unlike T or P, where the magnitude is directly interpretable.

At constant T and P, the Gibbs-Duhem equation (Eq. 10.14) states:

Σ xᵢ dμᵢ = 0   (const. T, P)

This means the chemical potentials cannot all increase or decrease simultaneously. If μ₁ rises as composition changes, at least one other μⱼ must fall, weighted by mole fractions. This is the thermodynamic coupling between species in a mixture — a constraint with no analogue in the temperature or pressure cases.

What this means practically

The phase equilibrium criterion μᵢᵅ = μᵢᵝ is the starting point for every VLE, LLE, and SLE calculation in chemical engineering. When you use Raoult's law, an activity coefficient model like UNIFAC or WilsonActivity coefficient models relate the chemical potential of a species in a real solution to its chemical potential in an ideal solution. The correction factor γᵢ captures non-ideal molecular interactions: γᵢ = 1 for ideal mixtures, γᵢ > 1 for repulsive interactions (positive deviations from Raoult's law), γᵢ < 1 for attractive interactions., or an equation of state for fugacity, you are always solving the same underlying problem: find the conditions under which the chemical potential of each species is equal in all phases present. The machinery varies; the criterion does not.

This is also why the partial molar Gibbs energyḠᵢ ≡ μᵢ. Every equation relating solution-phase thermodynamic properties has a corresponding partial molar counterpart. The chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs energy — the contribution of species i to the total G of the mixture, as it actually exists in solution. is the central quantity in solution thermodynamics, not the molar Gibbs energy of the pure component. A species in solution is not the same as that species in its pure form — its energy state is different, its interactions with neighbors are different, and its tendency to leave that phase is different. The chemical potential captures all of that in a single number.

The chemical potential of species i in a mixture is not a property of i alone. It is a property of i as it exists in that specific mixture at that specific composition, temperature, and pressure. This is why composition always appears explicitly in any expression for μᵢ in a real solution.
