Reaction Network Simulator

What it is

A process simulator that solves reactor networks end to end: 13 unit operations, isothermal / adiabatic / cooled thermal modes, and a chain running from material and energy balances through equipment sizing to installed-cost estimation. Cantera-linked property data; Turton correlations for costing.

The problem with a green test suite

229 tests passed. A passing test only confirms what I already thought to check. So I compared a recycle example against its analytic solution instead. With the recycle cut, the first-order PFR matched 1−exp(−kτ) = 0.776870 to six decimals. With the recycle connected, sweeping the split ratio from 10 % to 99 % left conversion pinned at 0.527633. A recycle stream that changes nothing is physically impossible.

Why the exact case mattered

Correctness without the recycle localised the fault immediately: the reactor models were right, so the defect lived in the loop.

Including the wrong turn

I read one line of code and concluded reactor volume floated under recycle. It did not — that line was the cooled-CSTR jacket calculation, unrelated to recycle semantics. Reading the code did not correct me; comparing numbers against the reference did.

What it changed

Verification is not the last step of building. The model is never the authority — the reference value is.

Two structural faults

One — the splitter never wrote to an edge already registered as a tear stream, so the recycle stayed frozen at its initial guess of unreacted feed. That is why conversion was lower than with no recycle at all: the loop was diluting the reactants. Two — convergence was being judged on intensive properties. A splitter divides molar flow without touching concentration, so that error was structurally forced to zero. The correct norm, ‖ΔF‖∞ over molar flows, was already written in the project’s own rules file — the implementation contradicted its documented contract.

Verification, and a second defect

Regression tests were written against the analytic solution rather than against the code. After the fix, agreement held to 5–6 decimals across the whole split-ratio range, and conversion converged to the CSTR limit of 0.600 as recycle grew. Verification then surfaced a second fault: divergence detection was aborting slow-but-valid convergence. Under Wegstein acceleration the same case converges in 66 iterations.

Case 02 · Organisational process

PALS — the bottleneck was the hand-off, not the language

Co-President, Peer Assistance & Learning Support, Sogang University · Mar 2025 – present

Condition

The organisation opened to external members that year. The executive team ended up mixing Korean-only speakers, English-only speakers, and members fluent in neither. Cross-team coordination took weeks; misunderstanding and friction repeated. The surface diagnosis was obvious — a language barrier.

Testing the diagnosis

I interviewed the leads of all four divisions — finance, events, content, HR — individually: what they handled, how, on what schedule, where they got stuck, and what was already working. The cause was not language. Nothing defined what had to be handed between teams, in what format, or when. With no fixed format, every hand-off was renegotiated from scratch — and negotiating from scratch in a second language costs double.

Intervention

I built a delivery structure rather than a language programme: standardised format and sequence for inter-team requests and hand-offs, so each team knew what to write and in what order. Personal rapport was treated as a separate problem and addressed through regular meetings.

Result

Cross-team coordination that had taken weeks resolved within hours.

Carried forward

When I meet a communication problem now, I check the path work travels along before I assess anyone’s language ability. Change the structure and the language barrier can be routed around.

Also on record

Came up through the organisation as a mentee. Outstanding Mentor Award (2024-1), Mentor of the Semester (2024-2), then authored the club’s first written constitution and its formal executive-transition system. 150+ mentees over four semesters.

Case 03 · Stakeholder judgement

When fair-looking rules are not fair

Samsung Dream Scholarship Foundation leadership camp, 2026 · planned jointly with the University Hope scholars’ council

Condition

I represented the Global Hope scholars. The two student councils had never worked together. The initial plan mixed Korean and international scholars into competitive recreation. Judged on rules alone it was fair — identical rules for both sides.

The asymmetry

The actual conditions were not identical. Korean scholars were more numerous, advantaged in the shared language, and shared more context. Competition does not damp such differences; it amplifies them. Equal rules and equal conditions of participation are different things.

Decision

The issue was raised with the foundation. After four hours of discussion we discarded the plan we had prepared and redesigned from scratch. What changed was not the programme content but the structure of interaction: recreation became ice-breaking; mixing activities were designed at individual level so team-strength gaps could not surface; team activities were deliberately left unmixed so neither side could dominate.

A disagreement resolved by reframing

On the character of the culture fashion show, I did not rebut the opposing view. I converted it into a shared design question — what experience should this segment create? — and submitted it in writing. Both sides aligned from there.

Carried forward

Discarding four hours of work is expensive, but a discussion that exposes the real problem is not a failure. I look at structure before rules now.
